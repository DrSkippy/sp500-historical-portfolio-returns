Our Results vs Published Research

How the Buy & Hold, Fractional Kelly and Insurance backtests compare with the academic and industry literature

← Back to Report | Summary Rebalancing & Kelly Insurance Horizon Risk Differences Sources | Buy & Hold ↗ Kelly ↗ Insurance ↗ Recent Returns ↗ Research ↗

Summary

This site backtests three families of strategy over every start date (3-day stride) of the S&P 500 price index from 1956 to 2026 and QQQ from 1999 to 2026, for holding periods of 1–15 years. We searched for published work that studies the same questions with comparable data. All three headline findings agree with that literature, with one caveat about how much any US-only history can say about long horizons.

  • Buy & Hold returns more than every constant-mix ("Fractional Kelly") variant, which in turn has less volatility and fewer losing start dates. This is what Kelly/Merton theory predicts when the growth-optimal stock fraction is above 100%, and in our data it is (1.39 for the S&P 500, 1.54 for QQQ). It also matches the rebalancing literature: rebalancing controls risk; it does not add return in a trending market. Agrees
  • Crash insurance at a 1.2%/yr premium loses to simply holding less stock. The premium is 2.3–9× what the policy actually paid out on the S&P 500. Studies of real index puts find the same thing: protection is priced above its expected payout (a volatility risk premium), so protected portfolios trail and reducing equity exposure is the cheaper hedge. Agrees
  • The share of losing start dates falls with horizon, to zero at 15 years on the S&P 500. US-only studies show the same pattern, but larger international samples and option-pricing arguments show long-horizon losses remain possible. Our 0% is a property of one fortunate, overlapping US sample, not a guarantee. Agrees, with caveats

1 · Rebalanced mixes vs Buy & Hold Agrees

The "Fractional Kelly" variants hold a fixed 75–90% in stocks and the rest in cash, and rebalance every 90 or 180 days. Kelly's criterion (maximize the expected log of wealth) applied to a lognormal stock and a risk-free asset gives Merton's growth-optimal stock fraction f* = (μ − r) / σ². Rotando and Thorp applied the Kelly criterion to the US stock market in 1992[1]. Using the same daily data the backtest uses:

Because f* > 1, every unlevered mix is below the growth-optimal fraction, so expected growth rises monotonically toward 100% stock. That is exactly the ordering in the backtest. The theory's growth gap between 100% and 75% stock on the S&P 500 is 0.33 points a year (7.07% vs 6.74%); the backtest's 15-year gap is 0.21 points (7.02% vs 6.81%).

Literature

  • Fractional Kelly (part Kelly portfolio, part risk-free) gives up some growth for much lower volatility and drawdown risk[2].
  • Perold and Sharpe: constant-mix rebalancing does best in volatile, trendless markets and worse than buy-and-hold in trending ones[3].
  • Vanguard (US stocks/bonds, 1926–2009): the goal of rebalancing is risk control, not higher return; monthly, quarterly and annual rebalancing give similar risk-adjusted results[4].

This backtest

  • Every Kelly variant has lower mean return and lower dispersion than Buy & Hold. On the S&P 500 at 10 years, 25% bonds cuts the losing share from 8.4% to 3.5% for 0.31 points a year of return.
  • 1956–2026 was a strongly trending market, so Buy & Hold winning on return is the Perold–Sharpe prediction.
  • 90-day and 180-day rebalancing differ by at most 0.02 points a year at any horizon on the S&P 500 (0.06 on QQQ): frequency barely matters, as Vanguard found.

2 · Paying for crash protection Agrees

The Insurance strategy buys a stylized policy that pays the loss beyond a deductible on the insured stock when the price falls sharply over 6 trading days, for a premium of 1.2% a year. Our parameter scan measured the fair premium: what the policy actually paid out per year of insured value over the full history.

Literature

  • Index puts are systematically overpriced relative to their payoff: expected put returns are below the risk-free rate, and at-the-money straddles lose about 3% a week[5].
  • Israelov, using the Cboe S&P 500 5% Put Protection Index (PPUT) from 1986: protection cut equity beta to about 0.74 with negative alpha (about −1.8% a year), and holding less stock produced the same return with lower drawdowns, lower volatility and a higher Sharpe ratio[6][7].
  • Cboe's own figures: PPUT's maximum drawdown was 38.9% vs 51.0% for the S&P 500 over 37 years to June 2023, with lower annualized returns[8].
  • Harvey et al. (2019) put the cost of a passive at-the-money put program at roughly −3.9% a year over 1985–2018, as cited by Janus Henderson[9][10].

This backtest

  • On the S&P 500, where the 1.2% premium is 2.3–9× fair, every insurance variant trails both Buy & Hold and every Kelly variant at every horizon. The best case cuts the losing share by at most 0.6 points vs Buy & Hold; 25% bonds cuts it by up to 8.7 points. This is the "pathetic protection" result.
  • Lowering stock exposure (Kelly) beats insuring it, as Israelov found with real puts.
  • The one exception confirms the pricing story: on QQQ the 9% policy's fair premium (1.33%) is above the 1.2% charged, so it is underpriced insurance. That variant has the best 1-year mean on QQQ and no losing 15-year windows. A real seller would not offer it at that price.

One difference: a market put's price moves with implied volatility, so it costs most when a crash looks likely. Our premium is a fixed rate, so the backtest does not capture that, and real protection bought after a volatility spike would have cost more than our policy.

3 · Does risk fall with the holding period? Agrees, with caveats

The backtest's share of losing start dates for Buy & Hold on the S&P 500 falls from 26.7% at 1 year to 8.4% at 10 years and 0.0% at 15 years, and the dispersion of annualized returns shrinks from 15.8 to 3.7 points. That is the familiar "stocks for the long run" picture.

Literature

  • Fama and French bootstrap US monthly returns (1926–2016) and show that uncertainty about the expected return itself has a large effect on long-horizon outcomes, even though it barely matters at short horizons[11].
  • Anarkulova, Cederburg and O'Doherty (39 developed markets, 1841–2019) estimate a 12% chance that a diversified investor loses to inflation over 30 years. They argue the US record is short and affected by survivor and easy-data biases[12][13].
  • Bodie: the cost of insuring against earning less than the risk-free rate rises with the horizon, even if returns mean-revert. This is disputed but widely cited[14][15].

This backtest

  • The 15-year windows overlap heavily. 70 years of data hold fewer than five independent 15-year periods, so "0 of 6,647 windows lost money" is much weaker evidence than it looks.
  • Losses here are nominal; a real (inflation-adjusted) loss count would be higher, especially for windows starting in the 1960s–70s.
  • QQQ already shows a 0.7% losing share at 15 years: the 11 losing windows all started between March and August 2000, at the dot-com peak. One badly timed entry is enough to break the pattern, as the international evidence warns.

Bodie's result also bears on the Insurance strategy: protecting a long horizon is expensive. The backtest sidesteps that by renewing a 90-day policy, so it insures short sharp falls, not slow bear markets like 2000–02 or 2007–09.

Where this backtest differs from the studies

  • Price returns only. The S&P 500 file is the price index and QQQ uses split-adjusted close. Most studies use total returns. For comparison, Damodaran's S&P 500 series, which includes dividends, is the standard long-run reference[16]. Excluding dividends lowers every strategy's return, and lowers the stock-heavy strategies most.
  • Cash earns the 1-year Treasury rate (annual average). Studies typically use 3-month T-bills or a bond index.
  • Nominal, pre-tax and cost-free. No inflation adjustment, taxes, bid–ask spreads or rebalancing costs. Costs would widen Buy & Hold's lead.
  • Stylized insurance. The policy pays on a 6-day price loss with a fixed premium, not a market-priced put. It is a cleaner thought experiment, but a real put's premium varies with implied volatility.
  • Overlapping windows from one market. Every start date 3 days apart is used, which gives smooth distributions but few independent observations at long horizons.
