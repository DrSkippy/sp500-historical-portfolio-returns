Insurance

Equity portfolio with a crash-triggered reserve — absorb sharp drawdowns without selling stocks

← Back to Report Buy & Hold Fractional Kelly Insurance Recent Returns ↗
Insurance 6 variants  ·  2 parameters  ·  ins_frac ∈ {5%, 10%}  ×  deductible ∈ {9%, 12%, 18%}

Hold nearly all capital in equities and insure the stock position against crashes. The policy costs a premium of 1.2% a year of the insured stock value. When the market drops sharply over a 6-day window by more than the deductible, the policy pays the loss beyond the deductible on the insured stock into a small cash reserve, and the portfolio rebalances. The portfolio also rebalances every 90 days.

Motivation

The largest single threat to a long-term equity investor is not gradual underperformance — it is a catastrophic drawdown that forces a behavioural exit at the bottom. The 2000–02 dot-com bust, 2008–09 financial crisis, and 2020 pandemic crash each saw the S&P 500 fall 30–55%. Investors who sold during those drawdowns locked in losses and often missed the recovery.

Traditional diversification (bonds, gold) reduces drawdown severity but also permanently caps upside. The Insurance strategy attempts a different trade-off:

  • Keep almost all capital in stocks to maintain equity upside during normal markets.
  • Insure the stocks, like buying crash protection: a policy that pays out only when a sharp crash occurs.
  • Pay an ongoing premium on the insured value in exchange for a payout precisely when equity losses are largest.

The question the backtest answers is whether that protection is worth its premium.

How It Works

📅

Day 1

Buy (1 − ins_frac) × capital / price shares — the insured asset. The remaining ins_frac × capital is a cash reserve that pays the premium and earns the prevailing Treasury rate.

📉

Every trading day

Charge the premium on the insured stock value. Compare today's price to the price 6 trading days ago; if the 6-day loss ≥ deductible, the policy pays out.

💰

Payout event

The policy pays the insured loss beyond the deductible into cash, and the portfolio rebalances the same day. The policy is then used up until the next scheduled rebalance renews it.

🔄

Every 90 days

Rebalance back to the target stock / cash split and renew the policy.

Payout trigger and size — the policy insures the stock, not the cash

loss_frac     = (price_today − price_6_days_ago) / price_6_days_ago
insured_value = shares × price_6_days_ago

if loss_frac ≤ −deductible and policy is active:
    payout = coverage_ratio × insured_value × (|loss_frac| − deductible)   # coverage_ratio = 1
    cash  += payout
    # then rebalance immediately; policy inactive until the next 90-day rebalance
    price_history.reset()

Example: $1,000 of stock falls to $250 (a 75% loss) with an 18% deductible. The policy pays 1,000 × (0.75 − 0.18) = $570 — the same whatever the size of the cash reserve. You absorb the first 18% ($180); the policy covers the rest.

Premium and cash

premium (per day) = premium_rate × shares × price / 365     # premium_rate = 1.2%/yr
cash             ×= (1 + treasury_rate) ^ (days_elapsed / 365)

The premium is 1.2% a year of the insured stock value — $1 a month per $1,000 insured — charged from the cash reserve. The cash itself earns the prevailing Treasury rate, like the bond side of Fractional Kelly.

Rebalancing

The rebalance routine is shared with Fractional Kelly: after a payout or at each 90-day interval, the total portfolio value (cash + stock value) is computed and shares are bought or sold to restore the target stock_frac = 1 − ins_frac split. After a payout this puts most of the payout back into stocks at depressed prices.

Parameters Explored

Parameter Values tested What it controls
ins_frac 0.05, 0.10 Fraction of the portfolio held as the cash reserve; the rest is the insured stock. The payout depends on the insured stock, not on the reserve, so a larger reserve mostly means less equity exposure.
deductible 0.09, 0.12, 0.18 Minimum 6-day price decline that triggers a payout; the policy pays only the loss beyond it. A lower deductible fires more often and pays more per event. A higher deductible reserves the payout for true crash events only.
rebalance_period 90 days (fixed for all Insurance variants) All Insurance variants rebalance — and renew the policy — quarterly. This is fixed to isolate the effect of the ins_frac and deductible parameters.
premium_rate 1.2%/yr (fixed) Annual premium as a fraction of the insured stock value ($1/month per $1,000).
coverage_ratio 1.0 (fixed) Fraction of the loss beyond the deductible that the policy pays.

The 2 × 3 = 6 variants span a range from Ins 5% ded9% (small reserve, fires on moderate crashes) to Ins 10% ded18% (larger reserve, fires only on severe crashes).

Deductible intuition

Thinking of the 6-day window: a 9% decline in 6 trading days is already an extreme event, and an 18% decline in 6 days is historically rare even in the worst market panics. The three deductible values bracket different regimes:

  • 9%: Fires during large corrections. More frequent payouts, and the most of each loss is covered.
  • 12%: Targets genuine crashes — 2008-style events.
  • 18%: Only the most severe crashes (early pandemic March 2020, October 1987). Very infrequent; the premium is paid for years between payouts.

Strengths & Limitations

Strengths

  • Maintains near-full equity exposure during normal markets
  • Crash payouts are countercyclical: cash arrives precisely when equity losses are largest, and the rebalance buys stocks at depressed prices
  • Payouts scale with the insured stock, so a small cash reserve still provides real protection against a sudden crash

Limitations

  • The 1.2%/yr premium on ~90–95% of the portfolio is a steady drag of roughly 1.1% a year, paid whether or not a crash occurs
  • Only fast crashes pay: a slow bear market (even a 75% fall spread over a month) mostly stays under the 6-day deductible
  • One payout per policy period: after a payout, further losses are uninsured until the next 90-day rebalance renews the policy
  • 6 variants is a narrower exploration space than Kelly; the result is sensitive to the specific crash profile of the historical period

What the Data Shows

In the backtest results, Insurance variants:

  • Trail Buy & Hold on the S&P 500 at every horizon, by roughly 0.8–1.3 points of annualized return: the premium costs more than the payouts return.
  • On QQQ, mostly trail Buy & Hold (by up to ~1.9 points); the cheapest-to-trigger variant (5% reserve, 9% deductible) roughly matches it at short horizons, where QQQ's sharper crashes pay out more often.
  • Do not reduce the share of losing start dates much: on the S&P 500 every variant has more losing 10-year starts than Buy & Hold (9–12% vs 8.4%). Protection against fast crashes does not help with the slow bear markets that drive most long-horizon losses.
  • Trail Fractional Kelly on the S&P 500, but beat it on QQQ, where Kelly's bond allocation is a bigger drag on a faster-growing index.
  • Low deductibles (9%) generally do best among the Insurance variants, since they pay out more often for the same premium.

Could different insurance terms beat Fractional Kelly? A scan of premium, deductible and coverage finds that insurance only beats the best Kelly variant when the premium is at or below what the policy historically paid out — a price no seller would offer.
